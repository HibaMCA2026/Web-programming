Trees are perennial woody plants defined by a self-supporting, elongated main trunk that anchors into the soil through a complex root system and extends upward into a branching canopy. As the largest and longest-lived organisms on Earth, trees use this towering growth strategy to outcompete surrounding vegetation for sunlight, fueling energy production via leaf-based photosynthesis. Their biological anatomy acts as a dual-direction highway: internal xylem transport systems draw water and minerals up from the roots, while phloem layers distribute life-sustaining sugars down from the crown, all shielded by a protective layer of tough outer bark. Geographically and structurally, trees generally split into broadleaf deciduous varieties (like oaks and maples) that boast wide, flat leaves shed annually to survive freezing winters, and coniferous evergreen varieties (like pines and spruces) that possess resilient, needle-like foliage and reproduce using cones. Beyond their immense botanical diversity, trees serve as the ultimate ecological anchors, actively stabilizing global soil systems, manufacturing oxygen, storing carbon, and providing critical habitats for millions of terrestrial species.
